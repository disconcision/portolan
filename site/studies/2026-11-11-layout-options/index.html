Layout and edge options

The canvas can now be switched between ten ways of placing nodes and four ways of drawing arrows while a graph is on the board. This page shows them on three of Portolan's own modules and says what each is good and bad at.

Built and written by an agent in one session. Every picture is a headless Chrome screenshot (canvas 1500 × 1000, light theme) of Portolan serving its own source, taken after picking the module and framing it. The agent looked at each one; Andrew has not. The cost figures were measured with tools/layout-cost.mjs; the default layout's positions were compared before and after the cost work and are the same. Not verified: any of the new options through a paced agent run, dragging nodes under them, the canvas's dark theme, Firefox and Safari. The pictures were retaken later the same day at double pixel density (open one to read it at full size), after the graph's labels were shortened and the dot grid made quieter; the three modules had grown a little by then.

The switches

Two selects in the canvas header, next to pace, follow and fit: layout and edges. A change lays the current graph out again and glides to it. The choice is remembered, and ?layout=ledger&edges=metro in the address overrides it. From script: PortolanCanvas.layouts(), edgeStyles(), setLayout(id), setEdges(id).

The three modules below: Watcher (17 definitions, 44 nodes drawn), Graph (43 definitions with a submodule, 76 drawn) and CanvasGraph (51 definitions with three submodules, 113 drawn). In the first pictures very long type names overflowed. They no longer do: a type's text is now cut to 32 characters at most, and unnamed lists, options and tuples are drawn as small dots.

Layouts

layered default, Hazel's

Types in left-to-right layers by dependency and function flow, stretched to fill the pane. It keeps to the canvas principles, and on a small program it reads well. On a real module the stretch spreads a few dozen nodes across the whole board, a record's field types sit in a far column joined by long dotted lines, and the functions tangle around the hub type.

compact new

The same layers without the stretch. An unnamed type that is used in one place (a record's field type, a lone result) docks beside that place instead of taking a row in a far layer, and the separate pieces are packed in source order into rows shaped like the pane. Better than the default where the default scatters, about the same elsewhere. On Watcher the islands close up. On Graph and CanvasGraph, which are mostly one connected piece, it comes out wider and flatter and no easier to read. It does nothing for the tangle of arrows around a hub type, and a piece that grows makes the pieces after it move.

ledger new

Each shared type heads a lane. What makes it sits in rows on its left, what is made from it in rows on its right, one function per row in source order, so a lane reads like a list of signatures: t ─ cycle → unit. A type with no name that is used in one place is a row of that place; a record's plain fields share rows, a few abreast. Lanes fill columns, a type after the types it is made from. The most readable of the ten for a module with one or two central types, and the only one where a function's name, input and result can be read off a row. Bad at: functions between two shared types, which become long arrows across lanes (the trunk lines in CanvasGraph); space, since lanes of different widths leave gaps; and stability, since a new function moves every row below it.

clusters new

The ledger's groups with the rows bent into arcs: what makes a type fans out to its left, what is made from it to its right. It is more compact than the ledger for a type with few functions. Not better than the ledger: labels on slanted spokes collide, neighbouring fans run into each other, and nothing lines up to be read down.

The lab's six Hazel's layout lab

These were compiled in but unreachable. function layers and type layers are the default with only one of its two reasons for a layer. packed groups is the default's own packing without the stretch. grid gives every host a cell of the same size. stable growth and soft anchors place new things around what was already there, so on a graph shown all at once they are the packing again. None of them changes what is hard about a real module. The lab's four engine layouts (ELK and CoLa) are not offered: they need a worker bundle of elkjs and WebCoLa that Hazel builds from its node_modules.

Edge styles

All four on Graph in the default layout, then two more on the ledger.

  • curves (default): horizontal-tangent cubics. Soft, and the label sits on the arrow. Several arrows out of one type leave on top of each other.
  • metro (new): level runs joined by short diagonals with rounded bends. Arrows that share a side of a node leave it side by side and turn in an order that keeps them from crossing each other; the label rides the longest level run. The best partner for the ledger, and in the default layout it separates the arrows out of a hub. It does not route around nodes, so a run can pass through one.
  • straight (new): chords from rim to rim; arrows between the same two nodes bow apart. Honest about distance and direction, and good for a fan. Long chords cross everything in their way.
  • circuit (the lab's router): right-angled routes that avoid nodes and each other. Tidy on a small graph; on a module the detours add more line than they remove crossings.

Layout cost

A render asked for the same graph in up to four frames and each was a full placement; and three passes searched lists by key inside loops over arrows and nodes. One placement now serves every frame, and those passes keep tables. The default layout's output is unchanged.

Ties

In Spall a tie gathers several cables into one bundle at a point. Each cable keeps a slot in the bundle's cross-section, the cables slide through the tie rather than being fixed to it, the bundle then moves as one heavier body, and a tie can be clamped to a post. Between two ties the run keeps its cross-section.

Here a tie would be a point on the board that a chosen set of arrows is routed through, side by side. It gives two things the canvas lacks. The first is manual layout for arrows: today only nodes can be dragged, and a tie is a draggable waypoint that several arrows share, stored the way drag offsets are (by name, as a delta from the automatic place). The metro style already draws arrows that share a side of a node as parallel tracks; a tie is that with the shared stretch placed by hand.

The second is the reading Andrew noted: a tie is a tuple made on the spot, of functions rather than of types. Tying three arrows out of t says "these three together", which is one arrow from t to a product; tying arrows into a type reads as a case over a sum; tying arrows that run in parallel between different ends reads as a product of functions. The canvas already has a node for a tuple of types (the filled ()). A tie would be its counterpart for functions, and it is the seed of something the source can hold: a record of functions, a module signature, the part of an interface one caller uses. A tie that has stayed for a while is a suggestion to name it.

Not built. The routing is a small step from the metro style; choosing arrows, placing and dragging the tie, and keeping it across changes are the work, and more than an hour of it.

Rough, and not done

  • Only the default keeps the canvas principles. The new layouts place everything afresh from the graph in source order: nothing jumps on a switch (it is one glide), but during an agent's run a new function will push its neighbours.
  • Metro and straight do not avoid nodes. In the ledger this rarely matters; in the layered layouts runs pass through nodes.
  • The ledger gives a terminal a row 42 px high because its label hangs below it. With the label beside the terminal the rows could be 28 px.
  • A submodule's hull follows its arrows, so in the ledger it smears across lanes.
  • The packing aims at the pane's shape but the camera does not magnify past 1:1, so a small module can leave part of the board empty.
  • Dragging a node works in every layout; the dragged offsets are dropped on a switch.
